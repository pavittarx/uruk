## Structure of Accounting

Accounting is a very specified and objectively laid subject an it works under a defined set of guidelines, policies, rules, agreements, covenants, and conventions. 

Hendrickson, 1977 holistically defines it as logical reasoning in form of set of broad principles that

1. provide a general frame of reference by which accounting practice can be evaluated.
2. guide the development of new practices and procedures.

It is evolutionary in nature, so it has to be governed with a basic set of core principles which would not only be standard point of reference for appraising, developing and reviewing but continually evaluating them too.

Accounting system relies on a foundation of concepts, standards, and principles to ensure reliable and consistent financial reporting. The core of accounting lies in the **accounting equation —**

**Assets = Liabilities + Capital**

This equation demonstrates the fundamental relationship between what a company owns (assets) and what it owes to others (liabilities and capital). This relationship forms the basis of the **double-entry bookkeeping system**, where every transaction impacts at least two accounts, ensuring that the equation always remains balanced.

**Key Components of the Accounting Structure:**
- Accounting Concepts
- Accounting Standards
- Accounting Principles

## Accounting Concepts
These concepts are fundamental ideas that provide a framework for recording and reporting financial information. They guide accountants in recognizing, measuring, and presenting financial data in a consistent and meaningful way.

### Concepts Observed at the Recording Stage:

These concepts guide how financial transactions are initially recorded. 

- **Business Entity Concept:** (Recognizing the business as a separate entity from its owner) 
This concept emphasizes that a business is a separate entity from its owner(s). This distinction is crucial for accurately tracking the financial performance of the business and separating personal transactions from business activities. For example, if an owner withdraws money from the business for personal use, it should be recorded as a reduction in owner's equity and not as a business expense.

- **Money Measurement Concept:** (Using a common monetary unit to measure and record transactions)
Accounting primarily deals with transactions and events that can be expressed in monetary terms. While this concept allows for a standardized measurement unit and facilitates comparisons, it also has limitations. For instance, it doesn't capture qualitative aspects like employee morale or brand reputation, which can significantly impact a business.

- **Objective Evidence Concept:** This concept highlights the importance of supporting all accounting entries with verifiable documentation. This documentation, such as invoices, receipts, and contracts, provides an audit trail and ensures the reliability and objectivity of financial information.

- **Dual Aspect Concept (**Acknowledging the two-sided nature of every transaction**):** 
This foundational concept states that every transaction has two sides - a debit and a credit - that must be recorded. This dual impact ensures that the accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Capital) always remains balanced. For example, purchasing equipment for cash increases the asset "Equipment" (debit) while decreasing the asset "Cash" (credit).

- **Historical Cost Concept:** (Recording assets and liabilities at their original cost.) 
According to this concept, assets and liabilities should be recorded at their original transaction price. This historical cost serves as the basis for all subsequent accounting for the asset or liability. It promotes objectivity as it relies on verifiable transaction data rather than subjective valuations.

- **Accrual Concept:** (Recognizing revenues when earned and expenses when incurred, irrespective of cash flow) 

This concept emphasizes that revenues and expenses should be recognized when earned or incurred, regardless of when the cash is received or paid. This approach provides a more accurate picture of a business's financial performance over a period by matching revenues with the expenses incurred to generate those revenues. For example, rent expense for December is recorded in December, even if the payment is made in January.

### Concepts Observed at the Reporting Stage:

These concepts influence how financial information is presented in financial statements

- **Going Concern Concept: (**Assuming the business will operate indefinitely**)** 
This concept assumes that the business will continue to operate indefinitely. This assumption allows for the allocation of costs over the useful life of assets (like depreciation) and influences how financial statements are prepared. However, if there's evidence that the business may not continue operating (e.g., impending bankruptcy), this concept might not apply, and assets might need to be valued differently.

- **Matching Concept:** (Pairing expenses with the revenues they generate.)
This concept, also known as the matching principle, states that expenses should be recognized in the same accounting period as the revenues they helped generate. This principle ensures that profits are accurately determined by linking revenues with the costs associated with earning those revenues. For example, the cost of goods sold is matched with the revenue from the sale of those goods in the same period.

- **Full Disclosure Concept:** (Providing all material information relevant to understanding the financial statements.) 
This concept emphasizes transparency in financial reporting. It requires businesses to disclose all material information that is relevant to understanding their financial position and performance. This disclosure often involves including additional notes and explanations alongside the financial statements.

- **Accounting Period Concept:** (Dividing the business's life into specific periods for reporting.) 
To measure financial performance periodically, this concept mandates dividing the continuous life of a business into specific time periods. These periods are typically monthly, quarterly, or yearly, allowing for regular reporting and analysis of financial information.

- **Conservatism Concept:** (Exercising caution by recognizing revenues when realized and anticipating potential losses.) 
  This concept advocates for caution in recognizing revenues and anticipating losses. Revenues should be recognized only when realized, and provisions should be made for all potential losses, even if their occurrence is uncertain. While this approach aims to prevent overstating profits or assets, excessive conservatism can distort the financial picture.

- **Materiality Concept:** (Focusing on information that is significant enough to impact decision-making.) 
This concept recognizes that not all financial information is equally important. It suggests focusing on information that is "material," meaning it could influence the decisions made by users of financial statements. It allows for practical considerations by omitting insignificant details that wouldn't significantly impact decision-making.

- **Consistency Concept:** (Applying accounting methods and procedures consistently over time.)
This concept promotes comparability and consistency in financial reporting by requiring businesses to apply accounting methods and procedures consistently from one period to another. Any changes in accounting methods should be disclosed and justified to ensure users can understand the impact of those changes.

The sources emphasize that while these accounting concepts are foundational, they are not always easy to apply in real-world scenarios. Accountants often need to exercise judgment and consider the specific circumstances when implementing these concepts. They also highlight that the dynamic nature of business and finance necessitates ongoing evolution and refinement of these concepts to maintain relevance and usefulness.

## Accounting Standards

While accounting concepts provide the basic framework for accounting, **accounting standards** offer a more structured and specific set of guidelines and rules. The need for accounting standards stems from the limitations of relying solely on broad concepts, which can be interpreted and applied differently by different companies.

These are specific guidelines and rules developed to ensure uniformity and comparability in financial reporting. They aim to:

- Provide a standardized framework for recording and presenting financial information.
- Reduce ambiguity and enhance the reliability of financial statements.
- Facilitate comparisons between different companies and across different time periods.

**The Purpose of Accounting Standards:**

The following are the objectives of accounting standards —

- **Standardization:** Accounting standards aim to bring consistency and uniformity to financial reporting practices. By providing clear guidelines on how to record and present financial data, they reduce ambiguity and enhance comparability across different companies and industries.

- **Reliability and Transparency:** By promoting standardized methods, accounting standards aim to enhance the reliability and transparency of financial statements. This, in turn, increases the trust and confidence of stakeholders, including investors, creditors, and regulators, who rely on this information to make informed decisions.

- **Comparability:** A key benefit of accounting standards, as highlighted in the sources, is facilitating comparisons between different companies and across different periods. When companies adhere to the same accounting standards, it becomes easier to assess their relative performance and make meaningful comparisons.

**Key Features of Accounting Standards:**

- **Developed by Authoritative Bodies:** Accounting standards are typically developed and issued by recognized accounting bodies, often with input from various stakeholders, including accounting professionals, regulators, and industry representatives. In India, this responsibility lies with the Accounting Standards Board (ASB) constituted by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI).

- **Specific Guidance:** Unlike broader accounting concepts, accounting standards provide more specific guidance on how to account for particular transactions and events. They address practical challenges and aim to reduce inconsistencies in how companies apply accounting principles.

- **Evolutionary Nature:** Accounting standards are not static; they evolve over time to reflect changes in business practices, economic environments, and the need for greater transparency and relevance in financial reporting.

**Benefits of Accounting Standards:**

There are several benefits of having widely accepted and implemented accounting standards —

- **Reduced Information Asymmetry:** By promoting transparency and comparability, accounting standards help reduce information asymmetry between companies and their stakeholders. This is particularly important for investors who rely on accurate and reliable financial information to make investment decisions.

- **Facilitates Global Business:** As business becomes increasingly globalized, having internationally recognized accounting standards, such as the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), becomes crucial. IFRS aims to create a common accounting language, making it easier for businesses operating in multiple countries to prepare and present their financial statements consistently.

- **Improved Resource Allocation:** When companies follow standardized accounting practices, it becomes easier for investors and lenders to assess their financial health and performance. This, in turn, can lead to more efficient allocation of resources as capital is directed towards companies that demonstrate sound financial management.

**Challenges in Implementing Accounting Standards:**

- **Resistance to Change:** Companies accustomed to particular accounting methods might resist adopting new standards, especially if it requires significant changes to their existing systems and processes.

- **Complexity and Interpretation:** Some accounting standards can be complex and require significant interpretation, potentially leading to inconsistencies in how they are applied by different companies.

- **Cost of Implementation:** Adopting new accounting standards, particularly for smaller companies, can involve costs related to training staff, updating IT systems, and engaging external consultants.

**Accounting Standards in India:**

India has been actively moving towards converging its accounting standards with the IFRS. This convergence process aims to enhance the quality and comparability of Indian companies' financial reporting, making them more attractive to global investors. The transition to IFRS, however, involves addressing various challenges, including training accounting professionals, aligning existing regulations, and managing the costs of implementation.

The critical role of accounting standards in ensuring reliable, transparent, and comparable financial reporting. They emphasize that while implementing standards involves overcoming certain challenges, the benefits in terms of improved financial reporting quality, enhanced stakeholder trust, and facilitated global business make it a worthwhile endeavor.

## Evolving Nature of GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles)

It represents a collection of commonly followed accounting methods, standards, and procedures accepted by the accounting profession. GAAP aims to establish a consistent framework for financial reporting, ensuring that information is presented fairly and accurately.

The accounting structure is not static. It evolves with the changing business environment and the need for greater transparency and comparability in financial reporting. The emergence of **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)** reflects this ongoing evolution, aiming to create a globally unified accounting language.

A strong accounting structure, built on well-defined concepts, standards, and principles, is crucial for businesses to communicate their financial performance effectively and maintain the trust of stakeholders.

GAAP encompasses the conventions, rules, and procedures that guide the preparation and presentation of financial statements.Here's an explanation of the changing nature of GAAP:

- **Dynamic Business Environment:** The business environment is constantly evolving. Factors such as globalization, technological advancements, changes in economic conditions, and new financial instruments necessitate continuous adaptation and refinement of accounting practices. GAAP must keep pace with these changes to remain relevant and ensure that financial reporting accurately reflects the underlying economic reality.

- **Need for Enhanced Comparability:** As businesses expand across borders and investors seek opportunities globally, the need for greater comparability in financial reporting has intensified. GAAP has evolved to address this need, particularly through initiatives aimed at converging national accounting standards with international frameworks like IFRS.

- **Increased Emphasis on Transparency and Disclosure:** The growing demand for greater transparency in financial reporting. Stakeholders are increasingly seeking more detailed and comprehensive information to assess a company's financial health, performance, and risks. GAAP has responded to this demand by emphasizing full disclosure of material information, including notes and explanations accompanying the financial statements.

- **Evolution of Accounting Standards:** While accounting concepts provide the foundational framework, the sources explain that accounting standards, which offer more specific guidelines and rules, are also constantly evolving. This evolution is driven by the need to address new accounting challenges, improve clarity, and enhance the relevance and usefulness of financial information.

- **Influence of Regulatory Bodies and Professional Organizations:** The role of regulatory bodies, such as the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) in the United States and the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), in influencing the development and implementation of GAAP. Professional accounting organizations, like the ICAI, also play a significant role in shaping accounting standards and best practices.

**GAAP is not a static set of rules but rather an adaptable framework that continually evolves to meet the changing needs of businesses and stakeholders. This evolutionary process is essential for maintaining the relevance, reliability, and comparability of financial reporting in an increasingly complex and globalized business environment.**

## Initiatives Towards Standardizing Accounting Practices

**Early Efforts in the UK:**

- **Formation of the Accounting Statements Committee (ASC):** Recognizing the need for greater consistency in financial reporting, the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales established the ASC in 1942. This marked a significant step towards addressing the inconsistencies and lack of standardized practices prevalent at the time.

- **Issuance of Exposure Drafts and Statements of Standard Accounting Practice (SSAPs):** The ASC adopted a collaborative approach by involving accountants from different companies to develop "Exposure Drafts" on specific accounting issues. These drafts were then circulated for public feedback, leading to the formulation and issuance of SSAPs, which provided formal guidance on acceptable accounting methods. This process aimed to establish a consensus and promote wider adoption of standardized practices.

**Developments in the United States:**

- **Establishment of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC):** In 1933, the US government formed the SEC, a regulatory agency tasked with overseeing the issuance and trading of securities. This move highlighted the importance of reliable and comparable financial reporting to protect investors and maintain market integrity.

- **Creation of the Accounting Principles Board (APB):** In 1957, the APB was established as a research-based entity with the responsibility of developing fundamental accounting principles. This marked a shift towards a more structured and research-backed approach to setting accounting standards.

- **Formation of the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB):** Building on the work of the APB, the FASB was formed in 1973 with the specific mandate of issuing statements and articulating GAAP. The sources note that the SEC's reliance on FASB pronouncements has significantly enhanced the credibility and influence of its accounting standards.

**International Standardization Initiatives:**

- **Establishment of the International Accounting Standards Committee (IASC):** Recognizing the need for global accounting standards, the International Congress of Accountants, held in Sydney in 1972, led to the formation of the IASC. The IASC aimed to harmonize accounting practices across different countries to facilitate cross-border investments and improve the comparability of financial statements.

- **Formation of the International Federation of Accountants (IFAC):** Furthering the global standardization efforts, the IFAC was established in 1978. While the sources don't elaborate on IFAC's specific role, its formation highlights the growing international recognition of the need for consistent accounting practices.

**Other Notable Efforts:**

- **European Economic Community (EEC):** The sources mention the EEC's contribution to standardizing accounting practices, particularly in areas like disclosure and consistency.

- **Canada:** The sources also acknowledge Canada's efforts towards promoting uniformity in accounting practices.

**Emergence of International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS):**

IFRS —  developed by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), IFRS aims to create a common global language for financial reporting, making financial statements more understandable and comparable across different countries. This ongoing global convergence towards IFRS signifies a substantial effort towards standardizing accounting practices on an international scale.

## International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)

IFRS is a globally recognized set of accounting standards aimed at harmonizing financial reporting across different countries.

**Purpose and Objectives:**

- **Common Global Language:** IFRS seeks to establish a "common global language of business affairs" to enhance the understandability and comparability of financial statements across international borders.

- **Facilitate Cross-Border Investments and Trade:** By promoting consistency and transparency in financial reporting, IFRS aims to increase investor confidence and reduce information barriers, fostering cross-border investments and trade.

- **Reduce Reporting Costs for Multinational Companies:** Companies operating in multiple countries often face the burden of preparing financial statements in accordance with different accounting standards. IFRS aims to streamline this process by providing a single, globally accepted framework.

**Key Features:**

- **Principle-Based Approach:** IFRS generally adopts a principle-based rather than a rule-based approach, emphasizing the importance of professional judgment in applying accounting standards to specific situations. 

- **Focus on Fair Value Measurement:** The sources mention that IFRS prioritizes fair value measurement for assets and liabilities, which may differ from the historical cost-based approach prevalent in some countries, including India. This emphasis on fair value aims to provide a more relevant and up-to-date view of a company's financial position.

**Development and Implementation:**

- **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB):** The IASB, an independent accounting standard-setting body, is responsible for developing and issuing IFRS.

- **Global Adoption and Convergence:** The sources highlight the increasing global adoption of IFRS. Many countries have either fully adopted IFRS or are in the process of converging their national accounting standards with IFRS.

**Benefits of IFRS Adoption:**

- **Increased Transparency and Comparability:** IFRS promotes transparency by requiring companies to disclose comprehensive information about their accounting policies and financial performance, making it easier for investors and other stakeholders to understand and compare financial statements across different companies and countries.

- **Improved Access to Global Capital Markets:** Companies following IFRS may benefit from improved access to global capital markets, as investors and lenders are more likely to invest in companies with financial statements prepared using a globally recognized accounting framework.

- **Enhanced Corporate Governance:** The emphasis on transparency and standardized reporting practices associated with IFRS can contribute to improved corporate governance and reduced information asymmetry between management and stakeholders. This is an inference based on common understanding of the impact of transparency in financial reporting.

**Challenges of IFRS Implementation:**

- **Training and Awareness:** Successful IFRS implementation requires significant training and education for accountants, auditors, and other financial professionals to ensure a thorough understanding of the standards and their application.

- **Regulatory Changes:** Countries adopting IFRS often need to revise their existing accounting regulations and legal frameworks to align with the requirements of IFRS.

- **Impact on IT Systems:** Adopting IFRS might necessitate changes to companies' IT systems and financial reporting software to accommodate the new accounting standards and reporting requirements.

IFRS represents a significant step towards harmonizing global accounting practices. While implementing IFRS presents some challenges, the benefits in terms of transparency, comparability, and access to global capital markets have made it a compelling framework for businesses and investors worldwide.

**Q:** A firm’s owner brings in an amount of INR 25 lakh as capital investment in his business. Share your understanding whether the firm has a corresponding liability towards the business owner, from the accounting’s business entity perspective?

- **Increase in Assets:** Cash Account - INR 25 Lakh
- **Increase in Owner's Equity/Liability:** Capital Account - INR 25 Lakh

**Check Your Progress 5**

**Q:** A firm buys a building in 2021 for INR 5 crore. The market was booming at that time, and by the end of the FY 2021-22, the market value of the building quoted Rs.7 crore. The company went ahead to appreciate the building in their book of records as INR 7 crore for the Financial year 2021-22. Share your opinion on this as an accounting professional, and how would you rate this practice?

**A:** Appreciating the building's value to INR 7 crore in the financial statements solely based on market fluctuations is **not advisable** and **generally not permitted** under standard accounting practices.

**Cost Concept:** The source emphasizes the Cost Concept, stating, "An asset is to be recorded in the books of accounts at the price for or at a cost incurred to acquire it." The building's purchase price of INR 5 crore represents its historical cost and should be the basis for its initial recording.

**Objectivity and Verifiability:** Recording the building at INR 7 crore based on market value introduces subjectivity. Market values can be volatile and open to interpretation. The historical cost is a more objective and verifiable measure.

**Exceptions:** It's important to note that there are exceptions to the historical cost principle. Certain accounting standards (like IFRS) allow or require the revaluation of specific assets (like property, plant, and equipment) to fair value if certain conditions are met and a reliable valuation method is used. However, even in those cases, the revaluation needs to be systematic and justifiable, not merely based on short-term market fluctuations.

**Q:** The year of a company (from the accounting perspective) gets over on 31st December every year. For this company, their premise rent amounting to INR 1,00,000 for the last quarter is delayed and thus couldn’t be timely paid for any reason. As an accounts professional, share your understanding of whether this due rental to be paid should be factored in the company’s book of accounts for closing purposes?

**A:** Yes, the unpaid rent of INR 1,00,000 for the last quarter should be factored into the company's books for closing purposes.

**Accrual Concept:** The source text discusses the Accrual Concept, which emphasizes recognizing expenses in the period they are incurred, regardless of when the cash payment occurs. Even though the rent payment is delayed, the company has consumed the benefit of using the premises for the last quarter.

Therefore, the accountant should make an adjusting entry:

**Debit:** Rent Expense Account - INR 1,00,000

**Credit:** Accrued Rent Payable (Liability Account) - INR 1,00,000

**Q:** A government furniture supplier dispatches various furniture goods to government departments. He finds that a few invoices totaling INR 50,000 were yet to be paid by said departments, and their due date has already surpassed on 31st March, i.e. the closing date of the yearly book of accounts. Now share your opinion, explaining whether the supplier should consider the revenue of INR. 50,000 in the closing year's account while evaluating the net profit of his firm?

**A:** Yes, the supplier should recognize the revenue of INR 50,000 in the closing year's account.

**Accrual Concept & Revenue Recognition:** Even though the payment is pending, the supplier has fulfilled its obligation by delivering the furniture. According to the Accrual Concept and Revenue Recognition principles, revenue is recognized when it is earned, not necessarily when cash is received.

The accountant should make an adjusting entry:

**Debit:** Accounts Receivable (Asset Account) - INR 50,000

**Credit:** Sales Revenue Account - INR 50,000

**Check Your Progress 9**

**Q:** A company is negotiating to get an order for Rs.5 lakhs from XYZ Company. It is confident to get an order, and as a result, it shows this order as a part of its sales revenue. Will you approve of such an accounting treatment of a probable order to be obtained in coming years?

**A:** No, recognizing the order as sales revenue before it is secured is **not appropriate** according to accounting principles.

**Conservatism Concept:** The Conservatism Concept, as discussed in the source, states, "anticipate no profit, provide for all possible losses." Recognizing potential revenue before the order is confirmed violates this concept.

**Revenue Recognition:** Revenue should be recognized when it is **earned** and **realizable**. Negotiations do not guarantee an order, and the revenue is not yet earned.

The company should wait until the order is confirmed and meets the revenue recognition criteria before recording it as sales revenue.

**Q:** A firm buys an office table for Rs. 800. Though it is, theoretically speaking, an asset having a life of more than one year, the firm shows it as an expense of the year and reduces the profit for the year. Is this accounting practice justifiable? Give reasons.

**A:** Yes, expensing the office table purchase immediately, even though it has a useful life of more than one year, can be justifiable.

**Materiality Concept:** The source text introduces the Materiality Concept. It suggests that items of insignificant value, even if technically classified as assets, can be expensed outright. This avoids unnecessary bookkeeping for items that have a negligible impact on the overall financial statements.

**Cost-Benefit:** The cost of tracking depreciation for an INR 800 table over several years likely outweighs the benefit of accurately allocating its cost over its useful life. Expensing it simplifies accounting and saves on record-keeping costs.

Accountants often establish a threshold for materiality. Items below this threshold are expensed, while those above it are capitalized and depreciated.
