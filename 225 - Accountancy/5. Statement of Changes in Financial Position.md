Statements of Changes in Financial Position

The SCFP (Statement of Changes in Financial Position) uses information from balance sheets and income statements to reveal the changes in a company's assets, liabilities, and shareholder funds over a specific period. The SCFP helps with policy formulation by reporting on the sources and uses of funds during an accounting period

**What the term funds mean to?** 
It may be noted that there are several concepts of funds, 
1. In narrow sense ‘funds’ refer to only cash and its equivalent and is used for expressing the liquidity of a business firm
2. In broader sense, it represents the working capital (Current Assets - Current Liabilities) of a firm.

## SCFP
Statements of Changes in Financial Positions, or SCFPs, are supplementary financial statements that provide a clearer picture of a firm's financial activities than balance sheets and income statements alone.  While a balance sheet reflects a company's assets and liabilities at a specific point in time, and an income statement details a company’s financial performance over a period of time, neither statement adequately explains the movement of funds over that time. An SCFP addresses this by identifying the sources and uses of funds during a given accounting period.

### Two Types of SCFPs
There are two types of SCFPs, each based on a different definition of "funds":
- **SCFP based on working capital**: This statement, also called a **Funds Flow Statement**, defines "funds" as **working capital**. This broad definition means that the statement includes all transactions that affect a business's current assets and current liabilities.
- **SCFP based on cash basis**: This statement, also called a **Cash Flow Statement**, uses a narrower definition of "funds," referring only to cash and cash equivalents. This statement focuses only on cash inflows and outflows and the transactions that affect a firm's cash position.

By analyzing the sources and applications of funds, businesses and analysts can gain a more comprehensive understanding of financial health than by consulting balance sheets or income statements alone.

## Funds v/s Flow of Funds
The terms "funds" and "flow of funds" are mostly used in context of the **Funds Flow Statement (which** analyzes changes in a company's working capital between two balance sheets).
### Funds
- **Broad Definition: Working Capital**
    Working capital represents the difference between current assets and current liabilities. In essence, it is the capital readily available to a business for day-to-day operations.

- **Narrow Definition: Cash**
   There also exists a narrower definition of "funds," where the term refers only to **cash and cash equivalents**. This definition emphasizes a company's liquidity—its ability to meet short-term obligations using readily available cash.
    
- **Importance of Context**
  The specific meaning of "funds" depends on the context. For example, when discussing the Funds Flow Statement, the term refers to working capital. However, when analyzing a company's ability to pay its bills, "funds" might refer more narrowly to cash on hand.
    

### Flow of Funds
"Flow of funds" refers to the movement of funds into and out of a business during a specific period, typically an accounting period. This movement is tracked and analyzed to understand how funds are generated and utilized.
- **Sources of Funds**: Transactions or activities that **increase** a company's funds. These might include issuing stock, obtaining loans, or selling assets.
- **Applications of Funds**: Transactions or activities that **decrease** a company's funds. This could involve repaying debts, purchasing assets, or distributing dividends.

### Significance of "Flow"
The "flow" aspect is crucial because it highlights the dynamic nature of a company's finances. It goes beyond static snapshots of assets and liabilities presented in balance sheets to reveal how financial resources are being generated and used over time.

By understanding the flow of funds, businesses can make informed financial decisions, such as managing working capital, investing in growth opportunities, or optimizing financing strategies.

## SCFP (Working Capital-Based): The Funds Flow Statement
**Statement of Changes in Financial Position (SCFP) based on working capital** is also called a **Funds Flow Statement**. 
- This type of statement defines "funds" as **working capital**, which is the difference between current assets and current liabilities.
- Working capital is the amount of capital that a business has available to meet its short-term financial obligations.

A Funds Flow Statement analyzes the reasons for changes in a company's financial position between two balance sheets. It highlights the sources and applications of funds (inflows and outflows) over a specific period, revealing how funds are obtained and used for various business activities. 

According to Robert N. Anthony, "The funds flow statement describes the sources from which additional funds were derived and the use to which these sources were put".

### Constructing a Funds Flow Statement
A funds flow statement is a statement showing the sources and uses of funds in a business firm during a certain period, generally an accounting period.
Constructing a Funds Flow Statement involves gathering the necessary information and then following a two-step preparing procedure.

### Sources of Information
1. Two comparative balance sheets (one at the beginning of the period and one at the end).
2. A summarized income statement (including non-fund and non-operating items).
3. A statement of retained earnings.
4. Supplementary information about changes in non-current accounts.

### Preparing Procedure
1. **Statement of Changes in Working Capital**: This statement compares the beginning and ending balances of current assets and current liabilities to show the net change in working capital. Current assets have a direct relationship with working capital, while current liabilities have an inverse relationship. An increase in working capital is an application of funds, and a decrease in working capital is a source of funds.

2. **Statement of Flow of Funds/Funds Flow Statement**: This statement analyzes the sources and applications (inflows and outflows) of funds. Sources of funds are transactions that increase working capital, while applications of funds are transactions that reduce working capital.

### Uses of Funds Flow Analysis
The Funds Flow Statement is a valuable tool for various stakeholders, including:
- **Management**: It helps in understanding the flow of funds, identifying areas of strength and weakness, and making informed financial decisions.
- **Shareholders**: It provides insights into the company's financial activities and their impact on working capital.
- **Bankers and Creditors**: It helps assess the company's short-term liquidity and ability to repay debts.

By focusing on working capital, the Funds Flow Statement offers a unique perspective on a company's financial health, complementing the information provided by balance sheets and income statements.

## SCFP Cash Basis: The Cash Flow Statement
A **Statement of Changes in Financial Position (SCFP) based on a cash basis** is also called a **Cash Flow Statement**. 

This type of SCFP focuses on a business's cash inflows and outflows during a specific period, revealing how cash and cash equivalents were generated and used.  Here cash refers to "cash in hand as well as demand deposits with banks," while cash equivalents are "short-term highly liquid investments" that can be quickly converted to cash without a loss in value.

Unlike a Funds Flow Statement, which focuses on changes in working capital, the Cash Flow Statement provides a more precise view of a company's liquidity.  A company with positive net income might still struggle to pay its bills if it has tied up too much cash in illiquid assets.

### Components of a Cash Flow Statement
A Cash Flow Statement is divided into three sections ——
1. **Cash Flows from Operating Activities:** These are cash inflows and outflows related to a company's primary revenue-generating activities. This section reveals the cash generated from a company's core business operations, such as sales and expenses.
2. **Cash Flows from Investing Activities:** This section reports cash flows related to the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets, such as property, plant, and equipment, and investments in other companies.
3. **Cash Flows from Financing Activities:** This section shows cash flows related to changes in a company's capital structure, including issuing and repurchasing stock, issuing and repaying debt, and paying dividends.

By analyzing each section of the Cash Flow Statement, businesses can gain valuable insights into how they generate and use cash and assess their overall financial health.

## Key Terms in Accounting Standard 3 (AS-3)
Some of the key terms used in Accounting Standard 3 (AS-3), "Cash Flow Statement," —
- **Cash:** AS-3 defines cash as "cash in hand as well as demand deposits with banks." This includes physical cash and readily available funds in bank accounts.
- **Cash Equivalents:** These are short-term, highly liquid investments that can be quickly converted to cash without significant risk of loss in value. AS-3 provides the example of government securities.
- **Cash Flow:** This refers to the movement of cash both into and out of a business during a specific period.
- **Cash Cycle:** AS-3 defines the cash cycle as "the period of time when cash is locked up in operations." It represents the time it takes for a company to convert its investments in inventory back into cash received from customers.
- **Operating Activities:** These are the cash flows directly related to a business's primary revenue-generating activities. Examples include cash receipts from customers and cash payments to suppliers and employees.
- **Investing Activities:** These involve the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets, such as property, plant, equipment, and long-term investments in other companies.
- **Financing Activities:** This refers to activities that alter a company's capital structure, including issuing or repurchasing stock, issuing or repaying debt, and paying dividends to shareholders.

Understanding these terms is essential for accurately preparing and interpreting a cash flow statement. The cash flow statement offers valuable insights into a company's liquidity and its ability to generate cash from its operations, investments, and financing activities.

## Constructing Cash Flow Statement
To understand how to construct a cash flow statement, it is essential to distinguish between two methods outlined in the sources: the direct method and the indirect method. Both methods classify cash flows into three main activities: Operating Activities, Investing Activities, and Financing Activities.  The key difference lies in how cash flows from operating activities are calculated.

### Direct Method
The direct method focuses on disclosing the gross cash inflows and outflows from a company's core business operations. For example, it would directly report cash receipts from customers and cash payments to suppliers and employees. This method offers a more transparent view of where cash is coming from and going to in a company's day-to-day operations.

### Indirect Method
The indirect method starts with a company's net profit (or loss) as reported in the income statement. Since the income statement is prepared on an accrual basis, meaning that it includes revenues earned but not yet received in cash and expenses incurred but not yet paid in cash, the indirect method makes adjustments to reconcile net profit with the actual cash generated from operations.

### Adjustments in the Indirect Method
1. **Non-Cash Items:** The indirect method adds back non-cash expenses deducted in the income statement, such as depreciation and amortization, as these expenses do not involve an actual cash outflow. Conversely, it subtracts non-cash income, such as gains on the sale of fixed assets, as this income doesn't represent cash inflows.
2. **Changes in Working Capital:** Changes in current assets and liabilities, such as accounts receivable, inventory, and accounts payable, also affect cash flow. An increase in current assets (other than cash) generally indicates a use of cash, while a decrease suggests a source of cash. Conversely, an increase in current liabilities usually represents a source of cash (as it defers cash outflows), while a decrease indicates a use of cash. The indirect method adjusts for these changes to accurately reflect the cash impact.

### Steps for Constructing a Cash Flow Statement (Indirect Method)
1. **Gather Information:** Obtain the company's comparative balance sheets (for the current and prior periods) and the income statement for the current period.
2. **Calculate Cash Flow from Operating Activities:** Begin with the net profit before tax, adjust for non-cash items, and then adjust for changes in working capital.
3. **Calculate Cash Flow from Investing Activities:** Analyze changes in long-term assets to determine cash inflows from sales and cash outflows from purchases.
4. **Calculate Cash Flow from Financing Activities:** Examine changes in long-term liabilities and equity to identify cash inflows from borrowings or issuing stock and cash outflows from repayments or dividend payments.
5. **Sum Up the Cash Flows:** Add the net cash flows from operating, investing, and financing activities to arrive at the net increase or decrease in cash and cash equivalents for the period.
6. **Reconcile Cash Balance:** Confirm that the net increase or decrease in cash matches the change in the cash and cash equivalents balance reported in the comparative balance sheets.

By following these steps and applying either the direct or indirect method, businesses can effectively construct a cash flow statement that provides valuable insights into their financial performance and liquidity.

The sources note that while listed companies in India must use the indirect method, both methods should ultimately yield the same net cash flow from operating activities.

## Exploring IND AS 7: Statement of Cash Flows
The sources provide limited information specifically on IND AS 7, "Statement of Cash Flows." However, they offer insights into cash flow statements in general, including AS-3, "Cash Flow Statement," which can be helpful in understanding IND AS 7.

### Key Points about IND AS 7:
- **Applicability:** IND AS 7 applies to all businesses, unlike AS-3, which exempts certain companies (e.g., unlisted firms with a turnover below a specific threshold).
- **Scope:** Similar to AS-3, IND AS 7 requires companies to prepare a statement of cash flows for each period for which financial statements are prepared. This statement should be an integral part of those financial statements.
- **Classification of Cash Flows:** IND AS 7 mandates that cash flows be categorized into operating, investing, and financing activities, aligning with the structure outlined in AS-3 and our previous discussion.
- **Limited Differences with AS-3:** The sources highlight that there are no substantial differences between AS-3 and IND AS 7.

### Similarities with AS-3 and IAS 7:
IND AS 7 likely shares several core principles with AS-3 and IAS 7:
- **Importance of Cash Flow:** All three standards underscore the significance of understanding a company's cash inflows and outflows as a critical aspect of financial reporting and analysis.
- **Three-Section Structure:** The classification of cash flows into operating, investing, and financing activities is a fundamental element common to AS-3, IAS 7, and, presumably, IND AS 7.
- **Methods for Operating Activities:** Both the direct and indirect methods for calculating cash flows from operating activities, as discussed in the context of AS-3, are likely applicable under IND AS 7 as well.

### Additional Insights Based on General Cash Flow Concepts:
- **Cash and Cash Equivalents:** IND AS 7 likely defines these terms similarly to AS-3, encompassing cash on hand, demand deposits, and short-term, highly liquid investments.
- **Disclosure Requirements:** Like AS-3 and IAS 7, IND AS 7 probably has detailed disclosure requirements to enhance the transparency and usefulness of the statement of cash flows. These might include policies for classifying cash flows, significant non-cash transactions, and other information relevant for users of the financial statements.
- **Focus on Cash Flow Analysis:** The mandatory application of IND AS 7 to all businesses underscores the significance of cash flow analysis for understanding a company's financial health and making sound business decisions.

## Applications of Cash Flow Analysis
The importance of **cash flow analysis** as a vital tool for financial assessment and decision-making. Here are some key uses of cash flow analysis:
- **Evaluating Liquidity and Solvency:** Cash flow statements reveal a company's ability to generate cash from its operations, which is crucial for meeting short-term obligations (liquidity) and ensuring long-term survival (solvency).
- **Assessing Cash Generation:** By analyzing the cash flow statement, stakeholders can determine whether a company's operations are generating sufficient cash to support its activities and growth. This insight is particularly relevant for investors and creditors.
- **Guiding Short-Term Financial Planning:** Cash flow statements are essential for short-term financial planning. They help management anticipate future cash needs, identify potential shortfalls, and make informed decisions regarding working capital management, debt repayment, and investment opportunities.
- **Evaluating Cash Management Efficiency:** Cash flow analysis helps management evaluate how effectively they are managing the company's cash resources. By analyzing cash inflows and outflows, management can identify areas for improvement in collecting receivables, managing inventory levels, and controlling disbursements.
- **Supporting Dividend and Expansion Decisions:** A company's cash flow from operations is a key factor in determining its ability to pay dividends to shareholders and fund expansion projects. Cash flow analysis helps management make informed decisions regarding these critical aspects of corporate finance.
- **Informing External Stakeholders:** Cash flow statements provide valuable information to external stakeholders, such as lenders, creditors, and investors. These stakeholders use this information to assess a company's creditworthiness, investment potential, and overall financial health.

Understanding a company's cash flow patterns is crucial for internal management and external stakeholders. By analyzing the cash flow statement, one can gain valuable insights into a company's financial performance, liquidity, and its ability to generate cash to support its operations and growth.
