# Introduction
- Cash Management, is also known as **treasury management** in corporate settings. 
- It is the process that involves collecting and managing cash flows from the operating, investing and financing activities of a company. 
- Treasury management is typically overseen by business managers, corporate treasurers, and chief financial officers. 
- **The Importance of Cash:** Cash is like the lifeblood of a business, essential for its smooth and continuous operation. However, it's also considered a **non-productive asset** because, unlike assets like fixed assets or inventory, it doesn't directly generate goods for sale.
- **The Need for Balance:** Maintaining the **optimal cash balance** is crucial. Having too much cash means funds are sitting idle, while too little can disrupt operations and hinder profitability.
- **The Cash Management Cycle:** This cycle involves a continuous process of generating cash from sales, using it for operating activities, investing surplus funds, and borrowing to cover any deficits. Effective cash management aims to complete this cycle at a minimal cost while ensuring liquidity and control.
- **Treasury Management's Evolving Role:** The role of a corporate treasurer has expanded beyond traditional cash management to encompass broader financial matters.
    - This includes securing internal and external funding, managing currencies and cash flows, and developing strategies to optimize the company's financial performance in a volatile and complex business environment.
    - Companies are increasingly focused on maximizing cash flow, effectively utilizing near-cash assets, optimizing capital structure and financing arrangements, managing treasury risks, and implementing efficient and controlled financial processes.

Cash Management is concerned w/

- Cash flows within the firm
- Cash balances (financing deficits & investment surplus)

The **Cash Management Cycle (CMC)** is a foundational concept. 
- It illustrates the continuous flow of cash within a company, encompassing sales generation, operating activities, investment of surplus, and financing of deficits .
- Effective cash management strives to complete this cycle with the lowest possible cost while ensuring the company maintains sufficient liquidity and control over its cash resources.

Sales generate cash which is used to pay for operating activities. Any surplus cash has to be invested, while the deficit has to be borrowed. Cash Management seeks to accomplish this at minimum cost. It also at the same time seeks to achieve liquidity and control.

The main problem of cash flows management arises due to difference in timing of cash inflows and outflows. In order to reduce this lack of synchronization, appropriate strategies for cash management should be developed. The key areas of focus for cash management strategies are 
1. Cash Planning
2. Managing Cash Flows
3. Optimum Cash Level
4. Investing Surplus & Financing Deficit

**Cash Planning** is used to prevent both cash deficits (which can hinder operations), and cash surpluses (missed investment opportunities). 
Cash budgeting is used for this purpose, which involves projecting future inflows and outflows, allowing companies to anticipate periods of potential surplus/deficit and take proactive measures.

**Cash Flows** involves strategically controlling both inflows and outflows. 
The primary aim is to **accelerate cash inflows** and, where possible, **delay cash outflows**.  This careful balancing act helps optimize the timing of cash movements to a company's advantage.

**Optimum Cash Balance** is a sweet spot that allows for holding  enough cash to meet obligations and ensure liquidity without tying up excessive funds that could be invested more profitably .
It requires carefully weighing the **cost of holding excess cash** (opportunity cost) against the potential costs associated with **cash shortages** (e.g., borrowing costs, missed investment opportunities, operational disruptions).
Baumol model, can be used to calculate optimum cash balance, especially when cash flows are predictable.

**Surplus and Deficit Cash Management** involves *investing these funds in short-term instruments to generate returns while maintaining liquidity*, when cash surplus arise. However, there should also be *plans for financing short-term cash deficits in advance to avoid disruptions when they occur.

## Motives for Holding Cash
The need for holding cash arises only when the cash inflows and outflows are not in sync. It can be either due to surplus cash or a deficit to meet the firm's requirements. Hence, the cash is held by firms to meet certain as well as uncertain situations.

A firm's need to hold cash can be attributed to these three motives
1. **Transaction Motive:** Firms need cash to involve in day-to-day operational expenses (purchases, wages, salaries, taxes, dividends, utility payments, etc.) .
		This motive is driven by the fact that cash inflows and outflows are not always perfectly synchronized. 
		Businesses need to have cash on hand to meet their financial obligations as they arise, even if their revenue streams are not consistent
		Firms usually do not hold large amounts of cash, instead the cash is invested in market securities whose maturity corresponds with some anticipated payments.

2. **Precautionary Motive** In order to mitigate uncertainties and emergencies (economic downturns, unexpected repairs, sudden shift in demand), a quantum of cash is held as precautionary objective. The amount of cash held can vary based on --
	1. The predictability of firm's cash flows - Businesses with more predictable cash flows may feel comfortable holding lower precautionary balances.
	2. The firm's ability to borrow at short notice - Companies with readily available lines of credit may choose to hold less cash for precautionary purposes.
		Companies chose to keep their precautionary balances in highly liquid and low-risk marketable securities to earn some returns while maintaining easy access to cash when needed.

3. **Speculative Motive** : Firms may hold cash to capitalize on profitable investment opportunities as they emerge. This motive is particularly relevant in volatile markets where prices are susceptible to sudden shifts in supply and demand.
		By holding cash, companies position themselves to act quickly when favorable opportunities present themselves, such as acquiring undervalued assets or investing in new projects.
## Cash Planning
Firms requires cash to invest in inventory, receivables, fixed assets, and payments for operating expenses, increase sales & earnings, and smooth operation of business.
There can be two types of situations in absence of proper planning --
1. Cash Deficit, this may hamper the normal functioning of the firm leading to liquidation.
2. Cash Surplus, this may lead to losing out on opportunities, keeping the cash idle or unproductive.
The main objective of cash planning is to reduce the possibility of idle cash (which lowers the firms profitability) and cash deficits (which can cause firm's failure).
Cash planning involves, developing a projected cash flow statement based on present and anticipated future operations.
### Cash Forecasting & Budgeting
A Cash budget is one of the most significant devices to plan and control cash receipts and payments. Following are the factors to be considered when doing a cash forecast --
    - **Credit Terms:** Both the credit period allowed to debtors (customers) and the credit period received from creditors (suppliers) are crucial for accurately forecasting the timing of cash inflows and outflows .
    - **Dividend and Tax Payments:** The timing and amount of planned dividend payments and tax liabilities must be factored into the forecast .
    - **Debt Repayment Schedules:** Forecasts need to incorporate scheduled principal and interest payments on existing debt obligations .
    - **Minimum Cash Balance:** Companies typically maintain a minimum cash balance to ensure liquidity. This target minimum level should be reflected in the forecast .
    - **Credit/Overdraft Limit:** Credit or overdraft limit allowed by the banks.
    - **Non-Cash Transactions:** It's essential to exclude non-cash transactions like depreciation from the cash flow forecast, as these don't directly impact cash movements .
## Determining the Optimum Cash Balance
A key responsibility for financial managers is determining the optimal amount of cash to hold. While holding cash is vital for various business operations, it is important to note that cash itself does not generate any return. Therefore, financial managers strive to strike a balance between holding sufficient cash and minimizing the opportunity cost associated with holding excess cash.
The finance manager has to ensure that enough cash is available for the  smooth running of operating activities as well as for paying of interest, dividends and  taxes. There should be availability of cash to meet the firm’s obligation as and when they become due.
The real dilemma which the finance manager faces is  to decide on the quantum of cash balance to be maintained in such a way that at any  given point of time there is neither cash deficit nor cash surplus.
- **Cost of Excess Cash:** The cost of holding excess cash is the potential return that could have been earned if those funds had been invested in profitable ventures. 
- **Cost of Cash Shortage:** A cash shortage can have significant consequences for a firm. It can impede daily operations, lead to missed investment opportunities, and may even necessitate costly emergency financing, potentially harming the firm's financial standing and reputation.

The firm should maintain optimum  just enough neither too much nor too little cash  balance. 
The Baumol Model, draws a parallel between cash management and inventory management. The model aims to minimize the total cost, encompassing both the cost of holding cash and the transaction costs involved in converting marketable securities into cash.

**Assumptions of the Baumol Model:** The Baumol Model operates under the following assumptions:
- The firm can forecast its cash needs with certainty.
- The opportunity cost of holding cash is known and remains constant over time.
- The transaction cost per transaction is also constant.

**Components of the Baumol Model:**
1. **Holding Cost:** This refers to the opportunity cost of holding cash, which represents the return the firm forgoes by not investing those funds. In the model, this is calculated as: $$\text{Holding Cost} = \text{I (C/2)}$$
		where, 
		$I - \text{interest rate earned on marketable securities}$ 
		$C - \text{amount of marketable securities converted into cash per cycle}$.

- **Transaction Cost:** This cost arises each time the firm converts marketable securities into cash.
			$$\text{Transaction Cost} = \text{B (T/C)}$$
		where,
		$\text{T/C} - \text{total number of transactions in a given period, typically year}$ 
		$T - \text{Fund Requirement}$ 
		$C - \text{Cash Balance}$.
		$B - \text{Constant per-transaction cost}$ 

**Total Cost & Optimal Cost Balance:**
The Baumol Model combines these costs into a total cost (TC) equation
		$$ \text{Total Cost (TC)} = Holding Cost + Transaction Cosr $$	                                  $$ TC = \text{I (C/2) + B (T/C)}$$
	where,
		$C - \text{amount of marketable securities converted into cash per cycle}$.
		$I - \text{interest rate earned on marketable securities}$ 
		$T - \text{Projected Cash requirement during the period}$
		$TC  - \text{Total Cost / Sum of conversion and Holding Costs}$

 To determine the optimal cash balance that minimizes this total cost, the model uses the following equation --
 $$ C* = \sqrt{\dfrac{\text{2BT}}{I}} $$
where,
	C - Optimal cash balance
	B - Transaction cost per conversion
	T - Total cash needs for the period
	I - Interest rate on marketable securities

**Limitations of the Baumol Model:**
- **Assumption of Certainty:** The model assumes perfect certainty regarding cash needs, which is not realistic in practice. Cash flows can be influenced by various unpredictable factors, making precise forecasting challenging.
- **Constant Opportunity Cost:** The model assumes a constant opportunity cost of holding cash, while interest rates can fluctuate in reality.
- **Constant Transaction Costs:** The assumption of constant transaction costs may not hold true, as these costs can vary depending on factors like the size and frequency of transactions.

**Practical Implications:**
Despite these limitations, the Baumol Model offers valuable insights for cash management. It underscores the importance of:
- **Accurately Forecasting Cash Needs:** While perfect certainty is unlikely, firms should strive to develop as accurate cash flow forecasts as possible.
- **Minimizing Transaction Costs:** By exploring cost-effective ways to transfer funds between marketable securities and cash, companies can reduce transaction costs.
- **Optimizing Cash Holdings:** The model emphasizes the trade-off between holding cash and investing it. By strategically managing their cash balances, firms can minimize costs and maximize returns.
# Methods of Cash Flow Budgeting
Cash budgeting is a tool for planning and controlling cash receipts and payments. A well-structured cash budget is crucial for businesses to anticipate and manage their short-term liquidity position effectively.

Cash flow is the lifeblood of any business. While accrual-based accounting provides a comprehensive view of profitability, it does not always accurately reflect the timing of cash inflows and outflows. A cash budget bridges this gap by providing a detailed projection of anticipated cash receipts and disbursements over a specific period.

- The cash budget is concerned  with liquidity it must reflect changes in opening and closing balances of debtors and  creditors. 
- It should also focus on other cash outflows and inflows.

**Key Considerations for Cash Budgeting**
● **Credit Terms**: Businesses need to factor in the credit terms they offer to customers and the credit terms they receive from suppliers. Understanding these terms is crucial for accurately projecting the timing of cash inflows from sales and cash outflows for purchases.

● **Major Payments:** The cash budget should incorporate significant cash outlays, such as dividend payments, tax installments, and debt repayments. Knowing when these payments are due helps avoid liquidity crunches.

● **Non-Cash Items:** It is essential to exclude non-cash items like depreciation from the cash budget. These items do not impact the actual cash flow of the business during the period.

● **Minimum Cash Balance:** Businesses generally maintain a minimum cash balance to meet unexpected expenses and opportunities. This minimum balance should be factored into the cash budget.

● **Bank Arrangements:** Businesses should consider any existing credit lines or overdraft facilities available from their banks to cover potential cash shortfalls.

● **Managing Surpluses and Deficits:** A cash budget helps businesses anticipate periods of surplus cash, allowing them to plan for short-term investments. Conversely, it helps identify potential cash deficits in advance, providing time to arrange for short-term financing.

**Benefits of Cash Flow Budgeting**
A well-prepared cash budget offers several advantages:
● **Improved Liquidity Management:** Helps businesses maintain adequate liquidity by forecasting cash surpluses and shortfalls.
● **Informed Decision-Making:** Provides insights into the timing and magnitude of cash flows, enabling better financial planning and decision-making.

# Investing Excess Cash
The fluctuations in working capital needs can sometimes result in a company having excess cash on hand. This excess cash can be due to seasonality of the product and business cycles, change in demand, etc. which must be invested  as -
- **Cash is Idle:** Excess cash doesn't generate any returns, making it important for companies to find ways to put these funds to work .
- **Meeting Future Needs:** While excess cash might be temporary, companies may retain a portion as a buffer for unforeseen financial requirements .

Factors to keep in mind when investing excess cash -
- **Safety:** The primary concern is minimizing the risk of default on both interest payments and principal repayment .
- **Maturity:** Short-term securities are generally preferred over long-term securities for investing excess cash. This is because the prices of long-term securities tend to be more volatile in response to interest rate changes .
- **Marketability (Liquidity):** The ease, speed, and cost of converting an investment back into cash are important considerations. Highly marketable securities ensure that companies can quickly access funds when needed .

**Short-Term Investment Options in India:**
- **Treasury Bills:** Issued by the government, these are considered very safe and highly liquid .
- **Commercial Papers:** Issued by creditworthy companies, these offer higher returns than Treasury bills but carry slightly more risk .
- **Certificates of Deposit (CDs):** Issued by banks, CDs provide fixed returns for a specified period. Their negotiability contributes to their liquidity .
- **Bank Deposits:** A straightforward option, but returns are typically lower than other short-term investments .
- **Inter-corporate Deposits:** Companies with surplus funds can lend to other companies (often subsidiaries or affiliates) with strong creditworthiness .
- **Money Market Mutual Funds:** These funds invest in a diversified portfolio of short-term marketable securities, aiming for higher returns than bank deposits .

- **Current interest rate environment:** Interest rates significantly influence the relative attractiveness of different investment options.
- **Company's risk tolerance:** Some companies might be more comfortable with higher-risk, higher-return investments, while others prioritize capital preservation.
- **Specific cash flow needs:** The timing and amount of anticipated cash needs will impact maturity choices.
- **Tax implications:** Different investment instruments might have varying tax treatments, which should factor into the decision-making process.

# Cash Collections and Disbursements
Cash collection and disbursement within the broader context of cash management, emphasizing the concept of "float" and techniques to optimize cash flow.

**1. Collection Float:**
Collection float represents the time lag between a customer making a payment and those funds becoming available in the company's bank account . This delay ties up cash that could otherwise be used for other purposes.
The causes of such delay are -
	- Time taken in postal transmission
	- Time take by the company to process cheques and drafts.
	- Time taken by the banks to clear the cheques.

- **Techniques to Reduce Collection Float:**
    - **Concentration Banking:** For companies with geographically dispersed customers, establishing regional collection centers allows for quicker deposits and reduces reliance on a single central location .

    - **Lock Box System:** Customers send payments to designated post office boxes managed by a local bank. The bank collects, processes, and deposits the payments, speeding up the clearing process .

    - **Electronic Funds Transfer (EFT):** EFT eliminates physical checks and allows for nearly instantaneous transfer of funds, significantly reducing or eliminating collection float .

**2. Payment Float:**
Payment float occurs when a company issues a check but the funds haven't yet been deducted from its bank account. This creates a temporary increase in available cash.
Companies can strategically manage payment float to their advantage by:
    - **Timing Check Issuance:** Issuing checks as close to the due date as possible maximizes the float period.
- **Cautions:** Companies must exercise caution when managing payment float. This is because stringent regulations regarding dishonored checks and potential damage to a company's creditworthiness make it crucial to avoid overextending payment float.

**3. Beyond the Sources:** 
- **Cash Flow Forecasting:** Accurate cash flow forecasting is essential for effectively managing both collection and disbursement floats. By anticipating peaks and troughs in cash movements, companies can proactively optimize their strategies.
- **Technology's Role:**  Modern treasury management systems (TMS) offer sophisticated tools for automating and streamlining collection and disbursement processes, further enhancing efficiency and control.
- **Relationship Management:** Strong relationships with banks and financial institutions. These relationships can be crucial for accessing favorable terms, negotiating faster clearing times, and exploring innovative cash management solutions.
# Treasury Management
Treasury management is a dynamic and increasingly strategic function within modern businesses.
Treasury management is comprehensive management of a company's financial resources, encompassing everything from generating internal and external funds to managing cash flows, currencies, and financial risks .

**Drivers of Change:**
There are several factors contributing to the evolving role of treasury management:
- **Volatile Financial Markets:** Rapid fluctuations in interest rates and exchange rates demand specialized skills and proactive strategies to mitigate financial risks .
- **Growing Economies and Increased Competition:** Expanding economies often lead to a higher demand for funds, requiring treasury departments to secure financing at optimal costs and allocate resources effectively .
- **Need for Value Creation:** Treasury management is no longer just about managing cash; it's about actively seeking opportunities to maximize the return on financial assets and support corporate goals .

**Key Responsibilities of a Modern Treasury Department:**
- **Liquidity Management:** Ensuring sufficient cash flow to meet short-term and long-term obligations. This includes:
    - Optimizing cash collection and disbursement processes.
    - Managing working capital effectively.
    - Maintaining strong relationships with banks and financial institutions.
- **Funding and Capital Structure:**
    - Determining the appropriate mix of debt and equity financing.
    - Securing funding at the lowest possible cost.
    - Managing debt maturity profiles.
- **Risk Management:**
    - Identifying, assessing, and mitigating financial risks, such as interest rate risk, foreign exchange risk, and commodity price risk.
    - Implementing effective internal controls to minimize fraud and operational risks.
- **Investment Management:**
    - Optimizing the return on excess cash while ensuring safety and liquidity.
- **Financial Planning and Analysis:**
    - Developing accurate cash flow forecasts.
    - Providing timely and insightful financial reporting to support decision-making.

**Benefits of Effective Treasury Management:**
- **Improved Cash Flow and Profitability:** Accelerating cash inflows, optimizing cash utilization, and reducing interest expenses can directly enhance profitability.
- **Cost Reduction:** Streamlining processes, leveraging technology, and negotiating favorable banking terms can lower operational costs.
- **Enhanced Risk Management:** Identifying and mitigating financial risks protects the company's financial well-being.
- **Improved Decision-Making:** Accurate cash flow forecasting and timely reporting provide insights that support strategic decision-making.
