# Cost of Capital: Introduction
The **cost of capital** represents the minimum rate of return a company needs to earn on its investments to satisfy its investors' expectations, whether they invest through equity shares, debentures, term loans, or a combination of these.
- **Capital Structure:** Cost of capital is a major factor in determining a company's sound capital structure, which refers to the mix of debt and equity used to finance its operations.
- **Financial Management:** A finance manager should prioritize understanding the cost of capital associated with different financing options to make decisions that minimize the cost of funds and maximize shareholder wealth.
## Why Cost of Capital Matters? Guiding Financial Decisions for Businesses
Significance of Cost of Capital --
The critical role **cost of capital** plays in various facets of financial decision-making for businesses. It acts as a fundamental benchmark, influencing investment choices, capital structure decisions, and the overall financial health of a company.

- **1. Evaluating Investment Opportunities:**
    - **Investment Hurdle Rate:** The cost of capital serves as a minimum acceptable rate of return (also known as the hurdle rate) that any new investment project must meet or exceed to be considered worthwhile.
    
    - **Project Acceptance/Rejection:** If a project's projected return is lower than the cost of capital, it implies that the company would be better off investing its funds elsewhere, potentially in alternative projects or financial instruments that offer higher returns.
    
    - **Project Ranking:** In cases where multiple investment opportunities are being considered, the cost of capital helps rank these projects based on their potential profitability, allowing companies to prioritize those with higher expected returns relative to the cost of financing.

- **2. Shaping Capital Structure Decisions:**
    - **Optimal Debt-Equity Mix:** A company's cost of capital is significantly influenced by its capital structure, which refers to the proportion of debt and equity used in its financing mix. The sources highlight the importance of striking an optimal balance between these two sources to minimize the overall cost of capital.
    
	- **Debt Policy Design:** Understanding the cost of debt (interest rates, loan terms) versus the cost of equity (returns expected by shareholders) is crucial for designing a debt policy that balances the benefits of debt financing (tax advantages) with its potential drawbacks (financial risk).

- **3. Project Appraisal and Performance Measurement:**
    - **Project Viability:** By comparing a project's internal rate of return (IRR) to the cost of capital, companies can assess the project's financial viability. A project with an IRR higher than the cost of capital is generally considered financially sound.
 
	- **Return on Investment (ROI) Benchmark:** The cost of capital acts as a benchmark for evaluating the overall return on investment (ROI) generated by a company's operations. Consistently earning returns exceeding the cost of capital is essential for long-term growth and shareholder value creation.

- **4. Financial Communication and Investor Relations:**
    - **Investor Expectations:** The cost of capital reflects investors' expectations regarding the return they require for providing capital to a company, whether through debt or equity.

	- **Financial Health Indicator:** A company's cost of capital can signal its perceived risk and growth prospects to investors and creditors. A lower cost of capital often indicates lower risk and stronger financial health, making it more attractive to capital providers.

**In essence, the cost of capital represents the "opportunity cost" of investing in a particular project or business.** It prompts companies to constantly evaluate whether their investments are generating sufficient returns to justify the cost of financing, ensuring that they are putting their resources to their most productive use.

# Significance (Opportunity Cost) of Capital
The **opportunity cost of capital** represents the potential return that a company _forgoes_ when it decides to allocate its funds to a particular investment project, rather than investing in an alternative opportunity with a similar risk profile.
Instead of focusing solely on the explicit financial costs of capital (like interest rates or dividend payments), the opportunity cost concept highlights the importance of considering the _relative attractiveness_ of different investment options.

The opportunity cost of capital is "the incremental return on investment that a business foregoes when it elects to use funds for an internal project, rather than investing cash in a marketable security."

Investors and companies face choices about where to allocate their limited resources. 
When evaluating an internal project, they must compare its potential return to the returns they could achieve by investing in readily available marketable securities (like stocks or bonds) with a comparable risk level.
Imagine a company considering a manufacturing facility expansion that is projected to yield a 10% return on a 10 crore investment. Simultaneously, they have the option to invest the same amount in stocks with an expected long-term yield of 12%. In this scenario, choosing the factory expansion means forgoing the potentially higher 12% return from the stock market. The 2% difference represents the opportunity cost of capital in this case.

#### Challenges and Considerations
Calculating the opportunity cost of capital isn't always straightforward.
**Investment Timeline:** Projects may have different time horizons. A long-term internal project might eventually yield higher returns than short-term market investments, but the delay represents a form of opportunity cost.

**Return Variability** 
While a marketable security might offer a higher expected return, its actual returns can fluctuate significantly. Internal projects, though typically offering lower returns, may have more predictable cash flows. This uncertainty needs careful consideration when comparing investment options.

**Risk Assessment** 
Evaluating and comparing the risk associated with different investment opportunities is crucial for accurately determining the opportunity cost. Investing in a project with a significantly higher risk profile than a marketable security, even if it promises a slightly higher return, might not be a wise choice.

**In conclusion, the opportunity cost of capital emphasizes the need for a comparative approach to investment decisions.** 
It highlights that the "true cost" of choosing one investment option is not just its financial cost, but also the potential returns that could have been earned from the next best alternative. Companies and investors can make more strategic capital allocation decisions by carefully considering this opportunity cost.

# Component Costs of Capital:
The **component costs of capital**, which are essential for calculating the overall cost of capital for a company. The cost of capital for each source of funds used by a company (e.g., debt, equity, preferred stock) must be determined separately before combining them into a weighted average.

Investors provide capital to a company with the expectation of a return on their investment._ The component cost of capital represents the minimum return that a company must earn on the funds obtained from a specific source to satisfy the return expectations of its investors.

### Key Components and Their Calculation Methods
The primary components of a company's capital structure, along with their respective cost calculation methods -

##### 1. Cost of Debentures ($K_d$)
Debentures are long-term debt instruments issued by a company. The cost of debentures represents the return required by debenture holders.
The cost of debenture is the discount rate that equates the net proceeds from issue of debentures to the expected cash outflows in the form of interest and principal repayments. 
 
    $$
    \begin{align}
    P & = \sum^{\Large{n}}_{\large{t-1}} \dfrac{I (1-t)}{(1+k_d)^{\Large{t}}} \\
    & = \dfrac{F}{(1 + k_d)^{\large{n}}}
	\end{align}   
$$
        
        $K_d$ - Post-tax cost of debenture capital
        I  - Annual interest payment per debenture
        t  - Corporate tax rate
        F  - Redemption price per debenture
        P  - Net amount realized per debenture
        n  - Maturity Period

Shortcut method for calculating cost of debenture -
$$k_d = \dfrac{I \: (1-t) + \dfrac{F-p}{n}}{\dfrac{F + P}{2}}$$

ABC Limited issues 15% debentures with a face value of Rs. 100 each, redeemable after 7 years at a premium of 5%. If the company receives Rs. 95 per debenture and the corporate tax rate is 50%, the cost of debentures would be 8.92%.
        
#### 2. Cost of Term Loans ($K_t$) 
Term loans are loans obtained from financial institutions with a fixed repayment schedule. 
The cost of a term loan is calculated similarly to the cost of debentures but is typically simpler as redemption premiums are less common.
        
$$Kt = I\:(1 - t)$$
Where:
        $K_t$ - Cost of term loan
        I     -  Interest rate on the term loan
        t     - Tax rate

#### 3. Cost of Preference Capital ($K_p$)
Preference shares have a fixed dividend rate and priority over equity shareholders in receiving dividends and assets upon liquidation.
$$ K_p = \dfrac{D + \dfrac{F - P}{n}}{\dfrac{F + P}{2}}$$
        
Where:
	$K_p$  - Cost of preference capital
    D     -  Preference dividend per share payable annually
    F     - Redemption price per preference share
    P     - Net amount realized per share
    n     - Maturity period
    
    
Approximation Formula: approximate cost when redemption periods are long.
$$ K_p = \dfrac{D}{P}$$
        
XYZ Ltd. issues 15% preference shares with a face value of Rs. 100, redeemable after 10 years. If the company receives Rs. 95 per share, the cost of preference capital is 15.9%.
        
#### 4. Cost of Equity Capital ($K_e$)
Equity capital represents the funds invested by shareholders. 
Determining the cost of equity is more complex than debt as it involves estimating the return expected by shareholders, considering factors like risk and future growth. 

**a. Dividend Forecast Approach (Constant Growth):** 
Assumes dividends will grow at a constant rate.    
           $$K_{\large{e}} = \dfrac{D}{P} + g$$
Where:
            
            $K_e$ - Cost of equity capital
            D - Expected dividend per share at the end of one year
            P - Current market price per share
            g - Constant growth rate of dividends
If a share is priced at Rs. 136, the expected dividend is Rs. 14, and dividends are projected to grow at 9%, the cost of equity would be 19.29%.
            
##### b. Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) Approach
The required return on equity to the risk-free rate, the market risk premium, and the stock's beta (systematic risk).

$K_e = R_f + \beta_i (R_m - R_f)$
            
Where:            
    $K_e$ - Cost of equity capital
    $R_f$ - Risk-free rate of return (e.g., government bond yield)
    $β_i$ - Beta of the company's stock (measures sensitivity to market movements)
    $R_m$ - Rate of return on the market portfolio (e.g., stock market index)

If the risk-free rate is 10%, the market return is 12.5%, and the company's beta is 1.5, the cost of equity would be 13.75%.
            
#### c. Bond Yield Plus Risk Premium Approach
Adds a risk premium to the yield on the company's long-term bonds to account for the higher risk of equity compared to debt.
            $K_e$ = Yield on long-term bonds + Risk premium
            
The risk premium is subjective and considers factors like the company's financial leverage, industry risk, and growth prospects.
            
#### d. Earnings-Price Ratio Approach
A simplified method that uses the expected earnings per share (EPS) and the current market price.
$$K_e = \dfrac{E_1}{P}$$ 
Where:
	  $K_e$ - Cost of equity capital
        $E_1$ - Expected EPS for the next year
        $P$ - Current market price per share

#### 5. Cost of Retained Earnings (Kr) (from source)
 Retained earnings are profits reinvested in the business. The cost of retained earnings is considered equal to the cost of equity capital because shareholders expect the same return on reinvested profits as they do on new equity investments.
    $$K_r = K_e$$
        
#### 6. Cost of External Equity ($K_e$)  
External equity refers to issuing new shares to raise capital. The cost of external equity is typically higher than the cost of retained earnings due to flotation costs (expenses associated with issuing new shares).
    
Dividend Capitalization Model, considering flotation costs)
        $$Ke = [D1 / (P(1 - f))] + g$$
Where:
        
$K_e$   - Cost of external equity
$D_1$   - Dividend expected at the end of year 1
P      - Current market price per share
g      - Constant growth rate applicable to dividends
f       - Flotation costs as a percentage of the current market price

Approximation Formula (ignoring growth, but considering flotation costs) 
        $$K_e = k_e / (1 - f)$$Where,
    $K_e$ - Cost of external equity
	$k_e$ - Rate of return required by equity investors
    f - Flotation costs as a percentage of the current market price

If the required rate of return on equity is 20% and flotation costs are 6% of the issue price, the cost of external equity would be 21.28%.
    
#### Important Considerations When Determining Component Costs
- **Tax Treatment:** Interest payments on debt are tax-deductible, reducing the effective cost of debt financing. This tax advantage is reflected in the formulas for the cost of debentures and term loans.

- **Market Conditions:** Interest rates, market risk premiums, and investors' risk appetite can fluctuate, impacting the cost of capital. It's important to use current market data when making calculations.

- **Company-Specific Factors:** A company's financial leverage, growth prospects, industry, and overall risk profile influence its cost of capital. Higher risk companies generally have a higher cost of capital.

By carefully calculating the component costs for each source of financing, financial managers can determine a company's overall cost of capital, which is essential for making informed investment and financing decisions.

# Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

 **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)** concept is crucial for evaluating investment decisions and reflects the overall cost a company incurs for its financing.
 
WACC is the average rate a company expects to pay to finance its assets, considering the proportion of each financing source in its capital structure. It represents the minimum return a company must earn on its investments to satisfy its investors (both debt and equity holders).

#### Why is WACC important?
- **Investment Decisions:** Companies use WACC as a discount rate when evaluating the profitability of potential investments. Projects with a higher rate of return than the WACC are generally considered favorable, as they add value to the company.

- **Capital Structure Optimization:** By analyzing the WACC under different capital structure scenarios (varying proportions of debt and equity), companies can strive to minimize their cost of capital and maximize shareholder wealth.

- **Performance Evaluation:** WACC serves as a benchmark for assessing a company's return on invested capital (ROIC). Earning an ROIC higher than the WACC indicates the company is creating value for its investors.

#### Calculating WACC
1. **Determine Component Costs:** As discussed previously, calculate the cost of each financing source separately (e.g., cost of debt, cost of equity, cost of preferred stock).

2. **Assign Weights:** Determine the proportion of each financing source in the company's capital structure. This can be based on:
    - **Book Values:** The relative proportions of debt and equity as reported on the balance sheet.
    - **Market Values:** Using the current market values of debt and equity, which often provide a more accurate reflection of the company's current capital structure.
    - **Target Capital Structure:** The desired mix of debt and equity that the company aims to achieve in the future.

3. **Calculate Weighted Average:** Multiply each component cost by its respective weight and sum the results.

**Formula (from source):**

```
WACC = (Wd * Kd) + (We * Ke) + (Wp * Kp) + ...
```

Where:
- WACC = Weighted Average Cost of Capital
- Wd, We, Wp = Weights of debt, equity, and preferred stock, respectively
- Kd, Ke, Kp = Cost of debt, cost of equity, and cost of preferred stock, respectively

Imagine a simplified scenario where a company has the following capital structure and component costs:
- Debt: 40% weight; Cost of debt = 6%
- Equity: 60% weight; Cost of equity = 12%

WACC = (0.40 * 0.06) + (0.60 * 0.12) = 0.096 or 9.6%

This means the company's average cost of capital is 9.6%. It must earn at least this rate on its investments to maintain its current value.

**Limitations of WACC:**
While a powerful tool, WACC has limitations:
- **Assumptions:** It assumes a stable capital structure, which may not hold true, especially for growing companies.

- **Subjectivity:** Determining the cost of equity, particularly the risk premium, involves some degree of subjectivity.

- **Project-Specific Risk:** WACC represents the company's overall cost of capital and may not accurately reflect the risk of individual projects, which can vary.

**Conclusion:**
Understanding WACC is vital for financial decision-making. By accurately calculating WACC, companies can make informed choices about investments, financing, and capital structure to maximize value for their stakeholders.
