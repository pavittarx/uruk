# Unpacking the Time Value of Money
Time value of money (TVM)**, a fundamental principle in finance asserting that a sum of money is worth more today than an equal sum at a future date. This difference in value stems from the potential of money to earn a return through investments over time.

- **Rationale for TVM:** 
  Key reasons why money possesses time value:
    - **Investment Opportunities:** Money available today can be invested to generate returns, leading to a larger sum in the future. Conversely, delaying investment means forgoing potential earnings.
    - **Risk and Uncertainty:** Future cash flows are inherently uncertain. The longer the time horizon, the higher the probability of unforeseen events impacting those cash flows. This uncertainty adds a risk premium to the value of receiving money today.
    - **Inflation:** The purchasing power of money tends to erode over time due to inflation. A sum received in the future will likely buy fewer goods and services than the same sum today.
    - **Consumption Preference:** Generally, individuals and businesses prefer to consume goods and services in the present rather than postpone gratification. This preference for immediate consumption contributes to the time value of money.
    
- **Compounding and Discounting:** 
  These are two essential TVM techniques:
    - **Compounding:** This technique determines the future value (FV) of a present sum by factoring in the accumulation of interest earned over time, including interest on previously earned interest.
    - **Discounting:** The inverse of compounding, discounting determines the present value (PV) of a future cash flow. It involves calculating how much an amount receivable in the future is worth today, given a specific discount rate.

# Compounding: Future Value of Money
Compounding is the process of calculating the future value (FV) of an investment by factoring in the interest earned not only on the principal amount but also on the accumulated interest from previous periods. It embodies the principle of "earning interest on interest," which leads to exponential growth of an investment over time.

The **future value (FV)** of money, a critical aspect of understanding the time value of money. This process, known as **compounding**, calculates how much a present sum will grow to over a specific period, given a particular interest rate.

#### How Compounding Works
Imagine you deposit $1,000 in a savings account that pays a 10% annual interest rate. After the first year, you would earn $100 in interest (10% of $1,000), bringing your account balance to $1,100.
In the second year, you would earn interest on the new balance of $1,100. The interest earned would be $110 (10% of $1,100), resulting in an account balance of $1,210. This process continues over the investment period, with interest earning interest, compounding the growth of your initial investment.
#### The Basic Compounding Formula
The following equation for calculating future value:

$$FV = PV \:(1 + k)\:^n$$

Where:
- $FV$ = Future value in n years
- $PV$ = Cash today (present value)
- $k$ = Interest rate per year (as a decimal)
- $n$ = Number of years for which compounding is done

Rearranging the equation, we can also get --

$$ k = \sqrt[\Large{n}]{\dfrac{FV}{PV}} - 1$$

This formula highlights how the future value is determined by:
1. The initial investment ($PV$).
2. The interest rate earned on the investment ($k$).
3. The length of time the investment is held ($n$).

- $(1 + k)^n$, referred to as the **compounding factor** or the **future value interest factor (FVIF k, n)**, represents the growth factor applied to the present value. 
- Tables containing pre-calculated values for this factor, for various combinations of interest rates and time periods, are readily available.

#### Factors Influencing Compounding
- **Interest Rate (k):** A higher interest rate results in faster compounding and a larger future value.
- **Time Period (n):** The longer the investment period, the more significant the impact of compounding on the future value. Even small differences in interest rates or time periods can lead to substantial variations in future value due to the exponential nature of compounding.
- **Compounding Frequency (m):** Interest can be compounded annually, semi-annually, quarterly, monthly, or even daily. More frequent compounding leads to a higher future value, as interest is calculated and added back to the principal more often.

#### Key Takeaways
- **Time Value of Money:** Compounding vividly illustrates the time value of money principle, showing how money available today is worth more than the same amount in the future due to its earning potential.
- **Exponential Growth:** Compounding leads to exponential growth of an investment, as interest earned in earlier periods starts earning interest itself, accelerating the growth over time.
- **Investment Decisions:** Understanding compounding is crucial for making informed investment decisions. It allows investors to compare different investment options and assess the long-term growth potential of their investments.

#### Compounding Frequency
- Interest can be compounded at frequencies other than annually, such as semi-annually, quarterly, or even monthly. 
- More frequent compounding leads to a higher future value because interest earned in earlier periods starts earning interest itself sooner.
The formula for calculating future value with more frequent compounding is:
$$FV_n = PV (1 + \dfrac{k}{m})^{(m \times n)}$$
Where:
- **m** = Frequency of compounding within a year.

#### Effective vs. Nominal Interest Rates
- The  **effective interest rate** to account for the impact of compounding frequency. 
- The **nominal interest rate** is the stated annual rate, while the **effective interest rate** represents the actual rate earned after considering compounding.
- The relationship between effective and nominal rate of interest is given by,
  $$r = \left(1+ \dfrac{k}{m}\right)^m - 1$$
	  where,
		  **r** - effective rate of interest
		  **k** - nominal rate of interest
		  **m** - frequency of compounding per year

**Doubling Period**
The time period required to double the investment. There are two methods to find this period -
- One obvious way is to refer to the table of compound factor form from which this period can be calculated.
- Rule of 72: the doubling period is obtained by dividing 72 by interest rate. 
	  Example, for interest rate of 8%, the approximate time for doubling an amount would be 72/8 = 9 years.
- Rule of 69: this is more accurate rule, as per this rule. 
	  $$\text{Doubling Period} = 0.35 + \dfrac{69}{\text{Interest Rate}} $$
	 Example, For 10 percent interest, $0.35 + \dfrac{69}{10} = \text{7.25 years}$

#### Continuous Compounding
**Continuous compounding**, a theoretical scenario where interest is compounded with infinite frequency, resulting in the highest possible future value for a given nominal rate. The formula for continuous compounding utilizes the mathematical constant 'e':

$$FV = PV \times e^{\large(k \times n)}$$

**Impact of Variable Interest Rates:**
- Interest rates can fluctuate over time. 
- Financial instruments are designed in a way that interest rates are benchmarked to a particular variable and with change in that variable the interest rates also change accordingly.
- In such cases, the future value calculation becomes more complex, requiring the application of different interest rates for different periods:
$$FV = PV \:(1+k_1)\:(1+k_2)\:...\:(1+k_n)$$
	Example, For a 50,000 deposit, for a Fixed deposit of a year rolled over to 3 years. The interest rate of 1st year is 5%, p.a. and the expected rate of interest for the next two years is 6%, and 6.5% respectively. 
	$$
	\begin{align}	
	FV & = PV \: (1 + k_1)\:(1+k_2)\:(1+ k_3) \\
	  & = 50,000 (1+0.5)(1+0.6)(1+0.65) \\
	  & = 59,267.25
	\end{align}
  $$
- It must be noted that average interest rate **is not** $\dfrac{(0.5) + (0.6) + (0.65)}{3}$ 
- Average Annual Interest Rate = 
	$$
	\begin{align}
	k & = \sqrt[\Large{n}]{\dfrac{FV}{PV}} \\
	k & = \sqrt[\Large{3}]{\dfrac{59267.25}{50,000}} \\
	k & = \sqrt[\Large{3}]{1.185345} \\
	k & = 5.8315%
	\end{align}	  
  $$

Alternatively,
$$
	\begin{align}
		k &= \sqrt[\Large{3}]{(1 + 0.05) + (1+ 0.06) + (1 + 0.065)} - 1 \\
		k &= 5.8315
	\end{align}
$$

Interest rates are not always constant, which adds complexity to compounding calculations. Additionally, they emphasize the importance of the effective interest rate, which takes into account the compounding frequency and provides a more accurate measure of the actual return on an investment.

#### Key Takeaways
The process of determining future value is fundamental to understanding the time value of money and making informed investment decisions.
- **Money Grows Over Time:** Compounding demonstrates how money invested today can grow significantly over time, especially with higher interest rates and longer time horizons.
- **Compounding Frequency Matters:** More frequent compounding results in higher future values due to the effect of interest earning interest.
- **Effective Rate Captures Reality:** The effective interest rate provides a more accurate representation of the true cost or return on an investment compared to the nominal rate.
- **Variable Rates Add Complexity:** Calculating future value with variable interest rates requires a step-by-step approach, applying the appropriate rate for each period.

# Annuities: A Stream of Payments
An annuity represents a series of equal payments or receipts occurring at regular intervals over a specified period. These regular cash flows are a common feature in financial planning and investment analysis.

#### Key Characteristics of Annuities
- **Constant Payment (A):** Each payment within an annuity is the same amount, denoted as 'A' in the sources' formulas.
- **Fixed Intervals:** Payments are made at regular intervals, such as annually, semi-annually, quarterly, or monthly.
- **Specific Duration (n):** Annuities have a defined lifespan, meaning the payments occur for a predetermined number of periods, denoted as 'n'.

#### Types of Annuities
Two main categories of annuities based on the timing of the cash flows
1. **Regular Annuity (Deferred Annuity):** In a regular annuity, the cash flows occur at the **end** of each period. For example, a loan repayment schedule with payments due at the end of each month exemplifies a regular annuity.
	   $$ FVA_n = A \left[\dfrac{(1+k)^n - 1}{k}\right]$$
2. **Annuity Due:** In an annuity due, payments occur at the **beginning** of each period. A common illustration is rent payments, typically made at the beginning of each rental month.
		$$
			FVA_{n(due)} = A \left[\dfrac{(1+k)^n - 1}{k}\right] \: (1 + k)
	$$

	where, 
		$A - \text{Constant periodic cash flow}$
		$k - \text{Interest rate per period}$
		$n - \text{Duration of annuity}$
		$FVA_n - \text{Future value of annuity which has n periods}$
		
#### Preset Value of Uneven Series
Cash flows occurring over a period are often uneven. 
- Dividend declared by companies will vary year to year.
- Interest on loans will vary if charged on a floating rate basis.
- The preset value of a cash flow stream is calculated by 
		$$
		\begin{align}
		\text{PV}_n & = \dfrac{A_1}{(1+ K)} + \dfrac{A_2}{(1+ K)^2} + \: ... \: + \dfrac{A_n}{(1+ K)^n} \\ 
		\\
		& = \sum^{\Large{n}}_{\Large{t=1}} \dfrac{A_t}{(1 + k)^{\Large{t}}}
		\end{align}
	 $$
where,
	$PV_n - \text{present value of cash flow stream}$
	$A_t - \text{cash flow occuring at the end of the year}$
	$k - \text{discounted rate}$
	$n - \text{duration of cash flow stream}$

#### Shorter Discounting Periods
Cash flow may have to be discounted more frequently that once a year, semi-annually, quarterly, monthly or daily. The result is two fold -
1. the number of period increases
2. the discount rate applicable per period decreases

The formula for calculating the present value in case of shorter discounting period is 
$$PV = FV_n {\left[\dfrac{1}{1 + k/m}\right]}^{\Large{n/m}}$$

where, $m - \text{number of times per year discounting is done}$

#### Time Value and Annuities
Understanding the time value of money is crucial when dealing with annuities. Since annuity payments occur at different points in time, it's necessary to discount or compound these cash flows to make meaningful comparisons or calculate their total value.

**Applications of Annuities:**
The concept of annuities is widely applicable in various financial contexts:
- **Loan Repayments:** Mortgages, car loans, and many other types of loans often involve fixed payments over a set term, making them annuities.

- **Retirement Planning:** Regular savings contributions to a retirement account and the subsequent periodic withdrawals during retirement can be modeled as annuities.

- **Investments:** Certain investment products, like bonds, provide regular coupon payments over a specified period, resembling annuities.

**Key Formulas:**
There are formulas for calculating both the future value (FV) and present value (PV) of annuities, taking into account the type of annuity and the time value of money. These formulas are essential for:
- **Future Value of an Annuity (FVAn):** Determines the total amount accumulated in the future from a series of regular payments, considering the interest earned.
- **Present Value of an Annuity (PVAn):** Calculates the current worth of a stream of future payments, discounting them back to the present using a chosen discount rate.

By understanding the concept of annuities and their associated calculations, you can effectively analyze and compare different financial products and make more informed investment and financial planning decisions.
