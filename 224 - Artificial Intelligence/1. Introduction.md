## **A Brief History of Artificial Intelligence**

The concept of AI is rooted in ancient times, with Greek mythology exploring the idea of intelligent machines.  However, the formal study of AI as a field began much later.

- **Early Developments (17th-Early 20th Century):**
    - The 17th century saw philosophers like Descartes propose that animal bodies could be understood as complex machines.
    - Pascal's invention of the mechanical calculator in 1642 marked a technological step towards automated computation.
    - The 19th century witnessed George Boole's development of "binary algebra", which aimed to represent the "laws of thought" mathematically.
    - Charles Babbage and Ada Byron collaborated on programmable mechanical calculators, laying groundwork for future computing devices.
- **The Mid-20th Century: The Birth of AI**
    - The advent of electronic computers revolutionized the study of intelligence.
    - In 1943, McCulloch and Pitts presented a model of the brain using Boolean circuits, suggesting the possibility of neural networks for mathematical computation.
    - Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machines and Intelligence" provided a comprehensive framework for AI, introducing the Turing Test to assess machine intelligence and exploring problem-solving using heuristics.
    - The 1956 Dartmouth conference marked a pivotal moment where the term "Artificial Intelligence" was coined, and pioneers in the field gathered for the first time.
- **Developments from the 1960s to 1990s:**
    - The 1960s saw the development of early AI programs like the Logic Theorist (1956) and SAINT (1961), capable of performing logical reasoning and solving calculus problems.
    - The period between 1969 and 1979 witnessed the emergence of the first knowledge-based systems.
    - The 1980s marked a resurgence of interest in neural networks.
    - The 1990s saw advancements in machine learning, data mining, and natural language processing, among other areas.
- **The 21st Century and Beyond:**
    - The 21st century has seen a surge in AI applications, fueled by the rise of the internet and advancements in computing power.
    - AI is being utilized in diverse sectors such as healthcare, agriculture, transportation, and finance.

## **Components of Intelligence**

**Key components of intelligence** often focused on in Artificial Intelligence research:

- **Learning:** The ability to acquire new knowledge or skills through experience. The document provides various learning methods, including:
    - **Rote Learning (Memorization):** Direct storage of information with minimal interpretation, similar to remembering multiplication tables.
    - **Learning by Instructions:** Acquiring knowledge by incorporating new information into an existing knowledge base, akin to traditional teaching methods.
    - **Learning by Analogy:** Forming new understandings by relating them to previously learned concepts, a technique frequently employed in textbooks.
    - **Learning by Induction:** Drawing conclusions based on patterns observed in numerous instances. While often accurate, conclusions drawn from induction are not always guaranteed to be true. An example is assuming all cows have four legs based on observing multiple four-legged cows, even though exceptions like three-legged cows might exist.
    - **Learning by Deduction:** Arriving at conclusions based on irrefutable logical reasoning. In deductive learning, if the initial assumptions are true, the conclusion derived through deductive reasoning is always correct. This method is commonly used in mathematics.
    
    The document also categorises learning based on feedback mechanisms:
    
    - **Supervised Learning:** Involves learning from labeled examples of inputs and their corresponding outputs. Examples include image recognition, mapping environmental conditions to actions, and understanding how the world changes.
    - **Unsupervised Learning:** Requires the learning system to identify significant patterns and relationships in data without prior knowledge of inputs or expected outputs. An example is navigating an unfamiliar city without a map.
    - **Reinforcement Learning:** Agents learn through trial and error, receiving positive or negative reinforcement based on their actions. This method is suitable for situations where the desired outcome is known but the specific steps to achieve it are not.
    - Delayed-reinforcement learning is the process of determining how to behave in situations when rewards are contingent on previous actions
- **Reasoning:** The capacity to draw logical conclusions based on available information. Reasoning can be divided into two types:
    - **Deductive Reasoning:** Involves drawing conclusions where the truth of the premises guarantees the truth of the conclusion. An example is, "If it's raining, the ground is wet. It is raining, therefore the ground is wet."
    - **Inductive Reasoning:** Involves drawing conclusions based on observed patterns, but the truth of the premises does not guarantee the truth of the conclusion. For instance, concluding that all swans are white after observing numerous white swans is an inductive inference, as there might be unobserved black swans.
- **Problem-solving:** The ability to identify and implement solutions to challenges. AI systems are applied to a wide array of problems, such as:
    - Developing winning strategies for games like chess.
    - Recognizing individuals in images (facial recognition).
    - Planning sequences of actions for robots to perform tasks.
- **Perception:** The process of sensing and interpreting the environment through sensory input. Artificial perception enables tasks such as:
    - Autonomous vehicles navigating roads at moderate speeds.
    - Robots identifying and manipulating objects in dynamic environments, like locating and removing empty cans in an office.
- **Language Comprehension:** The ability to understand and interpret human language.
    
    The challenge in language comprehension stems from the fundamental difference between **natural meaning** (like inferring rain from clouds) and **language-specific meaning**, which relies on convention and shared understanding. For Example, we can take traffic signs as a "mini-language" where the meaning is established by convention, contrasting this with the complexity of comprehending full-fledged human languages like English.

## **Approaches to Artificial Intelligence**

There are four approaches to consider when evaluating the human-likeness of an AI entity: the **Turing Test**, the **Cognitive Modeling approach**, the **Laws of Thought approach**, and the **Rational Agent approach**.

- **The Turing Test (Acting Humanly):** This test, proposed by Alan Turing, aims to determine if a machine can exhibit intelligent behavior indistinguishable from that of a human. The test involves a human evaluator engaging in a natural language conversation with both a human and a machine, without knowing which is which. If the evaluator cannot reliably distinguish the machine from the human, the machine is considered to have passed the test.

![Untitled](224%20-%20Artificial%20Intelligence/Untitled%203.png)

- **The Cognitive Modeling Approach (Thinking Humanly):** This approach centers on replicating the processes of human cognition within a machine. It relies on techniques like introspection, psychological experiments, and brain imaging to understand how humans think and then translating these findings into computational models.
- **Thinking Rationally: The Laws of Thought Approach:** This approach focuses on leveraging the principles of logic and reasoning to create AI systems. It involves encoding logical statements and rules into algorithms, enabling machines to derive valid conclusions from given information. This approach, however, faces limitations in real-world scenarios where strict adherence to logic might not always be feasible or desirable.
- **Acting Rationally: The Rational Agent Approach:** This approach prioritizes the agent's ability to consistently make optimal decisions given its environment and goals. It acknowledges that logical reasoning is just one aspect of intelligence, and emphasizes adaptability and flexibility in decision-making, especially in situations where multiple paths or conflicting goals exist.

Finding the right balance between human-centered and logic-driven approaches is crucial in AI research. While human behavior can sometimes appear irrational or emotionally driven, distinguishing between human and rational behavior is essential.  Human-centered approaches, often relying on empirical methods like experiments, and rationalist approaches, combining mathematics and engineering principles, have both led to valuable advancements in the field.

### Criticism of Turing Test

The **Chinese Room Test**, developed by John Searle, is a well-known criticism of the Turing Test as a measure of machine intelligence. The Chinese Room Test posits that a system (System A) convincingly mimicking the qualities of another system (System B) doesn't mean that System A possesses those qualities.  For example, a man can convince people that he is a woman, but this does mean that he is capable of bearing children like a woman.

**The Chinese Room Test**

The Chinese Room Test takes place in a room with two windows. A scholar who knows English but not Chinese sits in the room with a type of Shakespeare encyclopedia. One page of the encyclopedia is written in Chinese characters and the next page provides an English translation of the Chinese page.  Chinese characters with questions about Shakespeare are sent through one window to the person in the room. The person looks through the encyclopedia and, when they find the exact sequence of characters sent through the window, they read the English translation on the next page, form an answer to the question, and write it down in English. Then, the person looks for the corresponding sequence of Chinese characters in the encyclopedia and sends those characters out the other window. Searle argues that even though the person acted as if they understood Chinese, they did not. Similarly, just because a system can mimic a quality doesn't mean it has that quality.

- **How to Measure if AI Makes Machines Act Human**
    
    To determine if Artificial Intelligence (AI) enables a machine to behave like a human, several approaches can be used:
    
    - **Turing Test:** Proposed by Alan Turing, this test evaluates a machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behavior indistinguishable from a human. In this test, a human evaluator engages in conversations with both a human and a machine, without knowing which is which. If the evaluator cannot consistently distinguish the machine from the human based on the responses, the machine is considered intelligent.
    - **Cognitive Modeling Approach:** This approach focuses on replicating human cognitive processes in machines. Researchers study human thinking through introspection, psychological experiments, and brain imaging, and then use this knowledge to develop AI models that mimic these processes.
    - **Rational Agent Approach:** This approach aims to create AI systems that consistently make the best possible decisions in any situation. These rational agents prioritize maximizing their chances of achieving their goals, considering all available information and potential consequences.
    
    **Criticisms and Considerations**

For instance, the Chinese Room Test argues that a machine passing the Turing Test doesn't necessarily indicate genuine understanding. It highlights that a system can mimic intelligent behavior without possessing true comprehension, just like a person using a dictionary to translate a language without actually knowing it.
    
    Furthermore, distinguishing between "human" and "rational" behavior is crucial. Humans can be irrational due to emotional factors. Therefore, a human-centered approach might involve empirical studies, while a rationalist approach could focus on mathematical and engineering principles.

## Machine Learning

## Deep Learning

**Deep learning**, a subfield of machine learning, draws inspiration from the structure and function of the human brain to process data and identify patterns for decision-making.  It excels in learning from unstructured or unlabeled data in an unsupervised manner. The foundation of deep learning lies in artificial neural networks (ANNs), which are algorithms designed to mimic the interconnected nature of the human brain.

**How Deep Learning Works**

Deep learning operates similarly to multilayer perceptron models, employing various models like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. While the specific workings of CNNs and LSTMs are beyond the scope of the provided information, understanding multilayer perceptrons can offer insights into deep learning models.

Initially hindered by limited computing power, deep learning gained traction as computational resources improved. It effectively handles vast and unstructured datasets known as big data, often sourced from social media, search engines, e-platforms, and multimedia channels. The sheer volume and unstructured nature of this data make it challenging for humans to process and extract meaningful insights efficiently.

**Deep learning models**, similar to multilayer perceptron models, utilize neural networks structured like the human brain.  Each layer in the neural network receives input from the preceding layer, processes it, and passes the output to the subsequent layer for further processing and decision-making. This hierarchical structure enables deep learning systems to process data non-linearly, contrasting with the linear approach of traditional machine learning systems.

**Deep Learning vs. Machine Learning**

While both deep learning and machine learning are prominent AI techniques for processing big data, deep learning distinguishes itself through its hierarchical neural network architecture. Machine learning systems typically follow a linear data processing approach, while deep learning's non-linearity allows for more complex pattern recognition and analysis.

Consider a fraud detection system as an example. A machine learning system might analyze parameters such as transaction amounts. Conversely, a **deep learning system**, while potentially using similar parameters, leverages a neural network for enhanced analysis. Each layer within the network contributes to the decision-making process. For instance, an input layer could handle parameters like sender information, social media data, credit scores, IP addresses, and other relevant factors. This information would then be passed through subsequent layers for further processing, culminating in the output layer's decision regarding potential fraud.

## Intelligent Agents

A software agent is a system which, when given a goal to be achieved, could carry out the details of the appropriate (computer) operations and further, in case it gets stuck, it can ask for advice and can receive it from humans, may even evaluate the appropriateness of the advice and then act suitably.

An **agent** can be thought of as an entity that acts, generally on behalf of someone else. More precisely:

- An agent is an entity that perceives its environment through **sensors** and acts on the environment through **actuators**.
- Some experts require an agent to be **autonomous** and **goal-directed**.

**Percept:** an input to the agent through its sensors, over a unit of time, that is sufficient to make sense from the input.

**Percept sequence:** a sequence of percepts, generally long enough to allow the agent to initiate some action.

A **computer agent** is a computer software that:

- Has autonomous control; i.e., operates under its own control
- Is perceptive; i.e., is capable of perceiving its own environment
- Persists over a long period of time
- Is adaptive to changes in the environment
- Is capable of taking over others' goals

There are two approaches for defining an agent:

- **Ascription**: An agent is expected to act on behalf of someone to carry out a particular task which has been delegated to it. To perform its task, the agent must have knowledge about the domain in which it is operating and the properties of its current user. This approach can be summarized as "Agent is what agent does".
- **Description of Properties**: An agent is an entity, which functions continuously and autonomously, in a particular environment, which may have other agents also.
    
    According to the second approach, an agent should have some or all of the following properties:
    
    - **Reactivity:** The ability of sensing the environment and then acting accordingly.
    - **Autonomy:** The ability of moving towards its goal, changing its moves or strategy, if required, without much human intervention.
    - **Communicating ability:** The ability to communicate with other agents and humans.
    - **Ability to coexist by cooperating:** The ability to work in a multi-agent environment to achieve a common goal.
    - **Ability to adapt to a new situation:** Ability to learn, change, and adapt to the situations in the world around it.
    - **Ability to draw inferences:** The ability to infer or conclude facts, which may be useful, but are not available directly.
    - **Temporal continuity:** The ability to work over long periods of time.
    - **Personality:** Ability to impersonate or simulate someone, on whose behalf the agent is acting.
    - **Mobility:** Ability to move from one environment to another.

**Task environments** (or **problem environments**) include all the elements involved in the problems for which agents are thought of as solutions. Task environments will vary with every new task or problem for which an agent is being designed. Specifying the task environment involves looking at different measures or parameters.

**PEAS (Performance, Environment, Actuators, Sensors):** 

—— a standard set of measures for specifying a task environment.

**Performance** — what is actually the expected performance,

**Enviornment** — the world around the agent

**Actuators** — entity through which agent may perform actions

**Sensors** — different entities through which agent will gather information about the enviornment.

There are two parts to an intelligent agent's structure:

- A (hardware) device with sensors and actuators in which that agent will reside, called the **architecture** of the agent.
- An **agent program** that will convert or map the percepts into actions.

Agents can be grouped into classes based on their degree of perceived intelligence and capability. These agents can all improve their performance and generate better action over time:

- **Simple Reflex Agents:** These agents have no internal state and react only to the current percepts in their environments. They have very limited intelligence, do not store or remember anything, and cannot make use of previous experiences. In summary, they do not learn.
- **Model-Based Reflex Agents:** These agents maintain some sort of internal state in the form of a function of the sequence of percepts recovered up to the time of action by the agent. They have a model of the task environment encoded in the agent's program. The model has knowledge about how the task environment evolves independently of the agent and the effects of the agent's actions on the environment.
- **Goal-Based Agents:** These agents are driven by the goal they want to achieve. Their actions are based on information about their goal in addition to other information in the current state. In order to achieve their goal, the agent needs some information regarding the goal which describes the desirable events and situations to reach the goal. The agent program would then use this goal information to decide the set of actions to take in order to reach its goal.
- **Utility-Based Agents:** These agents use a more general way to judge success (or happiness). Each state is assigned a number that serves as an approximate measure of its success in reaching the goal from the state. The agent can then choose the reachable state in the next move with the highest assigned number, indicating the best chance of reaching the goal.
- **Stimulus-Response Agents (Reactive Agents):** These agents do not possess internal, symbolic models of their environments. Instead, they act or respond to the present state of the environment in a stimulus-response manner.
- **Learning Agents:** These agents have an initial knowledge base that is manually coded into it at the start. As the agent operates, it learns from its actions and improves its performance in ever-changing environments.

Learning agents consist of four components:

- **Learning Component:** Gradually improves the performance of the agent based on the percepts and the feedback from the environment.
- **Performance Component:** The component from which all actions originate based on external percepts and the knowledge provided by the learning component.
- **Critic:** Provides feedback to the learning element on how well the agent is doing and determines how the performance element should be modified to do better in the future.
- **Problem Generator:** Suggests actions that will lead to new and informative experiences.

The purpose of embedding learning capability in an agent is that it should not depend totally on the knowledge initially encoded in it and on the external percepts for its actions. There may be various criteria for developing learning taxonomies:

- The type of knowledge learnt, e.g., concepts, problem-solving, or game-playing
- The type of representation used, e.g., predicate calculus, rules, or frames
- The area of application, e.g., medical diagnosis, scheduling, or prediction

**Stimulus - Response Agents** are a special category of agents that represent a special category of agents which do not possess internal, symbolic models of their environments. They are relatively simple and interact with other agents in basic ways. However, complex patterns of behavior may emerge when the ensemble of agents is viewed globally.
